A story of sport and values

How a young woman found in sport the discipline and values to build her future.

About this story: The account is representative of Aldeas Infantiles SOS' family-based care model. It does not identify a real person. The organization publishes real stories on its official site, with anonymized photos or with consent.

🏆
Sport · 2 min read

When training becomes a school

Lucía was eleven when she arrived at the village. She came with a small backpack and an almost uncontainable energy she didn't know where to put. At school she found it hard to sit still; at home, she struggled to get along with her sisters. Then one afternoon, one of the caregivers took her to try the athletics training run by the community programme.

It wasn't love at first sight. For the first few weeks she complained about the heat, the noise, about having to wait her turn to throw the ball. Something shifted when a coach — a programme volunteer — sat down with her after practice and asked, not about her technique, but how she was doing.

"She didn't tell me I had to win. She told me sport was a place to learn to listen to myself and to trust myself. That was different from anything I'd heard before."

Discipline that carried into school

A few months in, Lucía started arriving on time for training. Then, without anyone asking her to, she started arriving on time for class. Sport gave her an external structure that, little by little, became internal. Her grades improved. The fights at home became less frequent. Not because sport magically transformed her, but because it gave her a place where she felt seen, and a set of habits she could carry into the other parts of her life.

At fourteen, she made her school's team. At sixteen, she was called up to a regional tournament. But what mattered most to her wasn't the medals — it was the routine of an afternoon, the teammates at the club, the feeling of belonging somewhere.

What she learned on the field

When people ask what sport gave her, she doesn't talk about trophies. She talks about learning to lose, to get up after a bad day, to listen when her coach points out a mistake. She talks about waiting her turn, sharing a changing room, cheering a teammate who scored a goal she didn't touch.

These are small things, the kind that feel ordinary, but that weigh heavily in adult life. Today Lucía studies physical education at university and coaches a group of girls at her old village on weekends. When people ask her why, she laughs and says: because somebody did that for me.

What this story shows

In the Aldeas Infantiles SOS model, sport isn't an end in itself. It's one more tool — alongside family-based care, education, and emotional support — that helps each child and young person develop confidence, relationships, and a life project of their own.

Sport and recreational activities are part of the community programme and are coordinated with the school and the caregivers. More information in the programs section.
